Smart cut writes .mkv even when you fed it an .mp4

Why that happens, whether it has to, and exactly what changing it costs — with results from running the real thing on your own files.

The current behaviour
Deliberate, not a bug — but stricter than Smart cut needs

The rule was written for Lossless cut, which accepts absolutely any source and therefore needs a container that swallows anything. Smart cut copied that rule, even though it already refuses everything except H.264 and HEVC — both of which sit in an .mp4 perfectly happily.

What I verified
The .mp4 version is byte-for-byte the same picture

I ran the full Smart cut procedure on your real files and finished it both ways. The .mp4 and the .mkv contain identical video and sound data — same count, same sizes, same checksums — on H.264, HEVC, and 10-bit HEVC. Nothing is lost by ending in .mp4.

1 What you see today, and why

You drop in C:\Users\PC\Downloads\ep 1.mp4, pick Smart cut, export — and get 1.mkv. If those clips are headed for Premiere, After Effects or DaVinci, none of them will import an .mkv. Neither will a browser, Discord's inline player, or Windows' own Photos app in some configurations. You end up converting the files again before you can use them, which is the exact step Smart cut exists to avoid.

The cause is one line that maps an export preset to a file extension. Lossless cut and Smart cut share it, and it says "MKV". That's it — there is no probing, no per-file decision, no fallback. The comment above it explains the reasoning: a preset that copies the original footage instead of re-encoding it can be handed anything at all, and an .mp4 container refuses some of what turns up in anime releases. MKV never refuses.

That reasoning is sound for Lossless cut. It doesn't hold for Smart cut, because Smart cut already turns away anything that isn't H.264 or HEVC at 8 or 10 bit — it stops with a refusal message rather than exporting. Those are the two formats .mp4 was built for.

2 How Smart cut actually builds a clip

This matters because it's the reason there was ever a doubt. A Smart cut clip is not one continuous piece of video — it's two pieces glued together:

Piece 1 — re-made
From your exact chosen start up to the next point the original can be cut cleanly. Usually well under a second. This part is re-encoded, which is how the clip opens on precisely the frame you picked.
Piece 2 — untouched
Everything from that point to the end of the clip, copied straight out of the source file with no re-encoding at all. This is where the "no quality loss" comes from.
Glue
The two pieces are joined, then the sound — cut separately and also copied untouched — is laid alongside them.
Container
The finished result is written into a file. This is the only step under discussion. Today it's always .mkv.

The two pieces describe themselves to a player slightly differently, because one was freshly made and one came out of your source file. During the original build this caused a real failure: an earlier version glued them together in a way that made HEVC clips fail outright with a muxing error. That's why the code carries a warning comment about containers, and why I refused to answer your question by reasoning — I tested it instead.

One genuine catch I found

HEVC clips written to .mp4 get labelled in a way Apple software rejects. When ffmpeg puts HEVC into an .mp4 it stamps it hev1 by default. QuickTime, Final Cut, and some versions of Premiere only accept the hvc1 stamp — same video, different label, and they refuse to open the file. I confirmed the default is the wrong one, and confirmed that adding one flag on the final step fixes it with no re-encoding and no cost.

Practical effect: if this ships without that flag, HEVC clips would come out as .mp4 files that some editors still won't open — you'd have traded one import problem for another. H.264 is unaffected; it gets the correct label automatically.

4 The rule I recommend

The temptation is to write .mp4 whenever the audio can go in it, and fall back to .mkv when it can't. I tested that idea and it's dangerous. I threw nine different sound formats at an .mp4 — including FLAC, Opus, Vorbis, DTS and uncompressed audio, which have no business being in an .mp4 — and ffmpeg accepted every single one without a word of complaint. So "try it and catch the error" catches nothing. It would hand you .mp4 files that ffmpeg was happy to write and that your player or editor silently can't open.

So the rule has to be decided up front, not discovered. The safest one is also the simplest:

If the source file is…
Smart cut writes…
Why this is safe with no probing at all
.mp4 / .m4v / .mov
.mp4
The sound track is already living inside an .mp4 right now. It came out of one, so it can go back into one. The picture is H.264 or HEVC or Smart cut would have refused the job. Nothing to check.
.mkv / .webm / anything else
.mkv
Exactly what happens today. No change, no risk, no new behaviour to test.

What makes this rule worth choosing over the clever version: it needs no inspection of the file, so the app knows the answer before it opens anything. That matters more than it sounds — the app picks the output filename before it looks at the source, and the merge screen shows you the predicted filename in advance. A rule based on the file's own name is knowable at both of those moments. A rule based on the audio format inside the file is not, and would either need extra work everywhere or would show you a filename that turns out to be wrong.

5 What has to change

The mechanical part

  • The extension rule gains one input: which file this clip came from. Currently it only knows the preset name. (clips.rs:36)
  • Single-clip export — the naming step sits two lines below where the source is already opened, so it just needs the path passed in. (clips.rs:1440)
  • Merged export — names the output before it gathers the source list, so those ten lines swap order. If several sources are being merged, all of them must be .mp4-family or the result is .mkv. (clips.rs:1816)
  • HEVC labelling flag on the final write, so Apple software accepts the result.
  • The interface's copy of the rule, which predicts the filename on the merge screen, updated to match. (ClipExtractorPanel.tsx:3773)
  • Dropdown wording — "Saved as MKV" becomes something honest about keeping your source's format.

Proving it works

  • One existing automated check asserts Smart cut produces .mkv and would fail immediately — it becomes a pair of checks, one per source type. (clips.rs:1075)
  • No interface tests currently assert the extension, so nothing breaks there.
  • The hands-on verification tool always writes .mkv and would need an .mp4 mode to check the result as strictly as it checks .mkv today.

6 What could bite you

Old and new clips side by side likely The app avoids overwriting by checking whether a file of that name already exists — but it only checks the same extension. Export into a folder that already holds 1.mkv and you'll now get 1.mp4 next to it. Nothing is destroyed, but you get two files called "1" and have to remember which run is which. Worth deciding: either number past both, or leave it.
The two copies of the rule drift apart medium The app guesses the merged clip's filename before exporting, and the actual name is decided separately in the backend. If those two ever disagree you'd see one filename on screen and a different one on disk. It's cosmetic — the app uses the real path afterwards, not the guess — but it looks broken.

Two things this change is not responsible for

While testing I saw ffmpeg print warnings at the join between the two pieces — timestamp complaints on HEVC, and missing-reference complaints when decoding across the seam. These appear identically in today's .mkv output. They're a property of gluing a fresh piece onto untouched original footage, not of the container, and the clips play correctly regardless. I'm flagging them so they don't get blamed on this change later — and they may be worth a separate look on their own merits.

7 How you'd check it in five minutes

Open the clip extractor, load C:\Users\PC\Downloads\ep 1.mp4, pick Smart cut, export two or three clips, and look in the output folder: you want 1.mp4, not 1.mkv. Drag one straight into Premiere or Resolve — the whole point is that it imports without a conversion step. Then repeat with any .mkv source you have: those must still come out as .mkv, unchanged. Finally check the first frame is still exactly where you set the cut, since that's the feature's actual job and the part that must not regress.

8 The decision in front of you

Option A — recommended
Match the source: .mp4 in, .mp4 out

An afternoon's work. Removes a conversion step from every Smart cut export you feed into an editor. Zero change in behaviour for .mkv sources, so the risky half of the surface isn't touched at all.

Option B — cheapest
Leave it, fix the wording

The dropdown already warns you it saves as MKV. If it turns out you rarely push these clips into an editor, the honest move is to change nothing and spend the afternoon elsewhere.
