Frame interpolation: build it yourself, don't ship theirs

The tool you sent me can't be absorbed into your app without giving away your source code. The good news is you don't need it — you already own almost everything required.

1 The trap

⚠ Copying their code would force you to publish yours

TheAnimeScripter is released under AGPL-3.0. That licence has one job: anything built on it must also be published as open source, to everyone who receives it. Your app is MIT — you can keep it closed, sell it, do what you like.

Paste TAS code into your project and that stops being true. You'd owe every person who downloads your installer the complete source of your entire app. Not a technicality you can quietly ignore — it's the whole point of that licence.

So there were only ever two honest routes: ship their program alongside yours without merging it (which is exactly how you already handle ffmpeg — same situation, different licence), or build the feature yourself.

✓ What I found that settles it

The actual AI model is free to use commercially. TAS is just a wrapper around it.

Frame interpolation in TAS is powered by an open research model called RIFE. I checked its licence directly: RIFE's code and its trained model files are both MIT — the same permissive licence your app uses. Commercial use allowed. The only obligation is keeping their copyright notice in your project.

TAS didn't invent the interpolation. It packaged someone else's model. You can go to the same source they did.

2 Why building it is now the cheap option

Normally "write it yourself" means more work and more download size. Here it's the opposite, because of one thing I didn't expect:

Your app already installs everything the AI needs. The 3 GB your setup wizard downloads for GPU mode — the machine-learning runtime, the graphics card support files — is the exact same machinery frame interpolation runs on. It's sitting on your users' disks right now, doing background removal and scene detection.

Adding interpolation on top of it costs a model file of a few dozen megabytes. Bundling TAS instead would mean shipping a second copy of all that machinery, plus a second Python — because TAS runs on Python 3.14 and your app runs on 3.13, so they can't share one.

3 The thing that would ruin this feature, and why you're already safe

Interpolation works by inventing the in-between frames of a motion. Feed it two frames from the same shot and it fills in the movement convincingly. Feed it the last frame of one shot and the first frame of the next, and it tries to invent motion between two completely unrelated pictures.

Within a shot — works

Across a cut — breaks

What you'd see: at every scene change, a few frames where one shot melts into the next like a bad dissolve. On an anime episode with a cut every two seconds, it's constant and obvious.

✓ Your app is structurally immune to this in its main use case

Clips exported from your scene grid are already cut at the scene boundaries. Each one contains a single continuous shot — there is no cut inside it to trip over. This is a real advantage TAS doesn't have, because it's handed whole episodes.

It only matters if someone drags an arbitrary file in. The spec still builds a guard for that — compare the two real frames, and if they're wildly different, hold the previous frame instead of blending — but since you've confirmed this is clips-only, it's now a silent safety net with no setting attached. It fires approximately never, so a knob for it would just be clutter.

Dropped from the earlier draft: the plan to feed your app's stored scene-cut list into the interpolator. That only earned its keep for whole-episode input. Gone.

4 What gets built

A new Frame interpolation section in the sidebar, shaped exactly like your background-removal feature — because that one already solves every hard part: a Python job with AI models, progress reporting, cancellation, GPU detection, CPU fallback.

Built around a queue, not a single file. You'll be handing it 10–40 short clips at a time, so the list of clips is the interface, with per-clip status and a running total.

Step 1
Drop in clips
Multi-select, or point at a folder and take everything in it.
Step 2
Four settings
Speed factor, model, output folder, and whether to overwrite or add a suffix. That's it.
Step 3
Watch two bars
The current clip, and how far through the queue you are. One clip failing doesn't stop the rest.
Step 4
Get the files
Audio preserved and in sync. Cancel any time — finished clips stay, the half-written one is cleaned up.
⚠ The one thing that must be right from day one

Load the AI model once for the whole batch, not once per clip. On a 4-second clip, starting up and loading the model takes as long as — or longer than — the actual smoothing. Do it per clip and a 30-clip batch runs several times slower than it should, with a progress bar that keeps resetting to zero. This is cheap to get right up front and painful to retrofit, so it's called out as a Phase 1 requirement with a test attached.

5 What you should expect it to feel like

Recent NVIDIA card

Works well. A short AMV clip finishes in the time it takes to make a coffee. This is the path the feature is designed around.

No graphics card support

Runs on the processor instead. It works, but it's slow enough that the interface must say so up front with a real time estimate — otherwise people assume the app froze.

Very large video

Can exhaust graphics memory. Phase 4 adds a resolution cap so it degrades gracefully instead of failing outright.

I've deliberately not put speed numbers in here. They depend entirely on your card and resolution, and a made-up figure is worse than none. Phase 1 ends with a real measurement on your machine.

Four things I'd want the implementer to not get wrong

  • Never copy a single line from the TAS folder. Even their version of the model code is a modified copy living inside their licensed project — taking it is the one irreversible mistake available here. The model must come from the original open-source project.
  • Don't touch the installed AI runtime. It's pinned to exact versions that your audio separation and scene detection depend on. Upgrading it to suit interpolation would break two working features.
  • Pin the model download to a permanent address. You've already been burned by this with ffmpeg — a download link that rotates gets deleted and every new install fails. Same rule here.
  • Keep the attribution. MIT is free to use but requires their copyright notice stays with your project. One small file, added before the first release that ships this.
