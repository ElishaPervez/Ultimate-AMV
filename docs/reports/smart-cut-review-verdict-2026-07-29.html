The Smart Cut review: is it worth acting on?

Short answer — yes. Every one of its three headline problems is real; I reproduced all three myself before touching any code. It also missed the single biggest one, which I found while fixing the others. Everything below is now fixed and committed.

Is the report useful
Yes — high quality

Seven concerns raised, seven real. It reproduced its claims with actual video files instead of reasoning from the source, and its numbers matched mine exactly when I repeated the experiment.

Is it complete
No — one blind spot

It tested a hand-written copy of what Smart Cut is supposed to do, not what the app actually runs. That copy worked; the app's version has been silently disabling itself on every single clip.

The one the review missed

8. The feature has been turning itself off on every clip

What you would have seen

Smart Cut exports finish, and the picture is right — so nothing looks broken. But they take as long as a normal export and the video gets squeezed a second time, because the part that was supposed to be copied straight out of the episode was being re-compressed instead.

The whole selling point of the preset — everything after the first fraction of a second is your original footage, untouched — was not happening at all.

Why

After copying the bulk of the clip, the app counts the pictures it got and compares that against what it asked for. If the count is wrong it assumes the copy grabbed footage from the wrong part of the episode and throws it away.

The counter was reading the tool's output slightly wrong for this one file type, so it always counted zero. Zero never matches, so every clip was declared suspect and re-encoded.

Why the review couldn't see it. Its checking tool rebuilds the Smart Cut procedure by hand and then inspects the result. Its hand-written version has no such counter, so it spliced happily and reported success — while the real app, given the same file, was quietly re-encoding. Two different programs were being tested, and only one of them ships.

I confirmed the miscount with the exact tool the installed app uses (version 8.1.1, the one sitting in your app's tools folder), not just the one on my path.

The three blockers, in plain terms

1. A clip that starts on the wrong picture

What you would have seen

You ask for the four seconds from 3s to 7s. You get five seconds that start a whole second early — the wrong shot entirely — and the export reports success.

It only happens with episodes whose internal clock does not start at zero. Plenty of rips and remuxes are like that; most files are not, which is why it slipped through.

Why

Two clocks were being compared as if they were the same one: the clip's times are counted from the first frame you can see, while the inspection tool reports times on the file's own internal clock. On a file that starts at 1.000, those two disagree by exactly one second.

The app concluded the cut already sat on a safe splice point when it did not, took the fast route, and landed a second early.

2 & 3. Merges that come out broken or silent

What you would have seen

Mixed sources: merge a clip from one episode with a clip from another that was encoded differently. The export succeeds, the file appears in your folder, and playback falls apart the moment it reaches the second clip. I reproduced this: the result claims to be one format throughout while the second half is another, and it decodes to nothing.

Silent first clip: if the first clip in the selection has no sound, the finished merge has no sound anywhere — the later clips' audio is dropped without a word. Put a clip with sound first and the same selection works. Also reproduced.

Why, and what I did

Joining pieces without re-encoding means the first piece decides what the whole file claims to be. Everything after it is copied in under that claim, whether it fits or not.

Smart Cut now inspects every source file before writing anything and refuses the merge if they disagree on format, colour depth, frame size, frame rate, or whether they have sound at all. You get one clear sentence naming the two files and pointing you at a re-encode preset, instead of a file that looks fine until you play it.

A deliberate choice you should know about. The review suggested building a full parallel sound timeline with silence inserted for the quiet clips. That is a real feature's worth of work and a new source of bugs. The review itself accepted refusing the merge as the alternative, so that is what I did: refuse clearly rather than half-solve it. Every anime rip has sound, so the silent case should be rare; if you ever hit the refusal in practice, tell me and I'll build the real thing.

The four smaller ones

4. Cancel that doesn't cancel

Cancel used to work by killing whatever was running at that instant. A Smart Cut clip runs five short jobs back to back, so there are gaps where nothing is running — a click landing in one of those gaps hit nothing and the export carried on to the next stage. Cancel is now recorded as a decision about the whole export: it is checked before each job starts and again after each finishes, and only a brand new export clears it.

5. Half-written files left in your export folder

A cancelled or failed export could leave a file with the exact name you were expecting, usually empty. Every final write now happens under a temporary name next to the destination and is renamed into place only once it has finished. If anything goes wrong, the temporary file is deleted and your export folder never gains a broken file.

6. The wrong-footage safety net had a hole in it

The check that catches a copy landing on the wrong part of the episode allowed the result to be a tenth of the clip off — two whole seconds of wrong footage on a twenty-second clip — and treated "couldn't measure it" as "it's fine". It now allows four frames, full stop, and an unmeasurable result forces the slow-but-correct route. This is the check that was also miscounting (problem 8); it now works and is strict.

7. The checking tool that could be fooled

Before

It compared the sizes of the compressed chunks. Same size counted as same content — so audio taken from completely the wrong moment could pass, since many sound formats produce equal-sized chunks throughout. It also failed a perfectly good export because 19 of 21 sizes matched and it demanded 20.

Now

It compares checksums of the actual compressed bytes, after putting both sides through identical processing so only real differences remain. Verified both ways: a genuine copy scores 72 of 74 chunks byte-identical (the two exceptions are the splice points, which legitimately carry an extra header), and a re-encode of the same footage scores zero. It also now takes the same "give up and re-encode" decision the app takes, so it tests the file the app would really produce.

What I checked, and what I did not

Verified myself
  • Problem 1 — built a file with an offset clock, measured 122 frames starting a second early before, 98 frames starting correctly after.
  • Problems 2 and 3 — built the mixed and silent-first merges, watched both "succeed" and come out broken.
  • Problem 8 — confirmed the miscount with the app's own bundled inspection tool.
  • The checking tool — now passes 5 of 5 on both a normal file and an offset-clock file, and correctly scores zero on a re-encode.
  • 132 backend tests pass, including 8 new ones covering the offset clock, the merge refusals, and the miscount.
Not done — deliberately
  • No run inside the real app. Everything above was proven with the same tools the app drives, on generated video. Exporting a real clip through the interface is yours to try.
  • Mixed sources still refuse instead of merging. See the note above.
  • Lossless cut has the same mixed-source weakness and I left it alone — it was outside what was reviewed, and changing it risks breaking merges that work today. Worth a follow-up.
